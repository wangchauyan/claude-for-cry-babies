# 7.1 What Is an MVP? (No Silicon Valley BS)

> *"MVP doesn't stand for 'Most Verbose Pitch deck.' It stands for build the damn thing already."*

---

## The Only Definition You Need

An MVP -- Minimum Viable Product -- is **the simplest version of your idea that someone else can actually try out.**

That's it. Not the simplest version you can *imagine*. Not the simplest version you can *describe*. The simplest version someone can **open, click on, and use.**

If you can't hand it to someone and say "here, try this" -- it's not an MVP. It's a plan. Plans are nice. MVPs are nicer.

---

## The Three Criteria

Your MVP needs to pass exactly three tests. Not thirty. Three.

| Criterion | What It Means | The Bar |
|-----------|--------------|---------|
| **It works** | Doesn't crash, doesn't freeze, doesn't show error messages | Someone can use it for 2 minutes without hitting a wall |
| **It looks decent** | Not beautiful, not award-winning -- just not embarrassing | You wouldn't cringe showing it to your boss |
| **It solves one clear problem** | Not five problems. Not "everything." ONE thing. | You can finish the sentence: "This helps you ____" |

That's the whole checklist. Three green checkmarks and you've got an MVP.

---

## The Core Spirit: Done Beats Perfect

Let's have a quick motivational reality check:

| Path A | Path B |
|--------|--------|
| Spend 6 months planning the perfect app | Spend 6 hours building an ugly-but-working tool |
| Beautiful wireframes, detailed specs | One Claude conversation, one Artifact |
| Show investors a pitch deck | Show your coworker a working prototype |
| "We're still finalizing the architecture" | "Here, try it" |
| Zero users after 6 months | Real feedback after 6 hours |

Path B wins. Every single time. In every industry. For every type of product.

A **working ugly thing built in a day** beats a **perfect plan that takes a year**.

Why? Because the moment someone actually *uses* your thing, you learn more in 5 minutes than you learned in 5 months of planning. They'll click something you didn't expect. They'll ask for a feature you never considered. They'll use it in a way that makes you go, "Oh. OH. *That's* what this should actually be."

You can't get those insights from a plan. You can only get them from a thing that exists.

---

## "But What If It's Not Good Enough?"

It won't be. That's the point.

Your first version will be missing features. The design will be basic. There will be edge cases you didn't think of. Someone will inevitably say, "Can it also do X?"

All of that is **fantastic**. It means people are engaging with your thing. They're imagining what it could become. They're investing mental energy in your idea. That only happens when there's something real to react to.

Nobody brainstorms improvements for a pitch deck. Everybody brainstorms improvements for something they just used.

---

## The MVP Mindset for This Chapter

Here's how to approach the projects in this chapter:

1. **Start with "what's the one thing this does?"** -- Not the feature list. The ONE thing.
2. **Build it in one Claude conversation** -- If it takes more than that, your scope is too big.
3. **Show it to someone within 24 hours** -- Accountability and feedback are magic.
4. **Write down what they say** -- Their feedback is your v2 roadmap.

You're not building a startup. You're building a thing that works. That's more than most people ever do.
